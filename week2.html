The week on one page

In one paragraph

A business earns revenue by selling at a price, and that price is set where buyers’ demand meets sellers’ supply. Elasticity tells you whether changing the price will raise or lower revenue. On the other side of the ledger, the production function turns inputs into output, and those inputs create costs. Because resources are scarce, every choice has an opportunity cost; counting it turns accounting profit into economic profit, and the PPF shows the trade-offs between products when resources are fixed.

Why it matters for you as an analyst, in the lecturer’s words from Lecture 3: firms use demand and supply data to forecast sales, set prices, plan production and inventory, and decide on new markets or products. Your job is to spot changing patterns in that data so managers decide from evidence rather than assumptions.

Part A: Recorded lectures 3–7

Markets and market structures

Summary

A market is any place, physical or online, where buyers and sellers meet to exchange a good or service. How many sellers there are decides who controls the price.

Organised vs unorganised. An organised market brings buyers and sellers together at a set time and place, like a mandi or farmers’ market. An unorganised market is scattered, like ice-cream vendors spread across a town.

By level of competition:

StructureSellersWho sets the priceExample from class
Perfect competitionMany, selling near-identical goodsMarket forces only; no single buyer or seller can move itCommodity markets
OligopolyA fewStrongly influenced by the few big playersIndian telecom (Airtel, Vodafone Idea and a handful of others)
MonopolyOneThe single seller has very high bargaining powerIRCTC for railway tickets; Indian Oil earlier in fuel

Perfect competition has two conditions: goods are the same or very similar, and buyers and sellers are so numerous that nobody can influence the price.

Demand

Definition

Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices during a given period of time.

  • Willing means there is a want or need. Able means there is purchasing power. Wanting a BMW you cannot afford is a desire, not demand.
  • At different prices: demand is a whole relationship, not one number.
  • Over a period of time: demand is always measured per week, month, and so on.

Law of demand. Ceteris paribus (other things remaining the same), when price rises, quantity demanded falls, and when price falls, quantity demanded rises. If a mug costs ₹50 you might buy five; at ₹100 you buy fewer.

A demand schedule is the table of prices and quantities. Plotting it gives the demand curve, with price on the y-axis and quantity on the x-axis. It slopes downward.

Market demand is the sum of every individual’s demand at each price. If A wants 4 cones and B wants 3 cones at a price of 2, and they are the whole market, market demand at that price is 7.

Supply

Definition

Supply is the quantity that sellers are willing and able to offer for sale at different prices during a given period of time.

  • Able means capacity. A plant that can make 10,000 units cannot sell 10,001, whatever the price.
  • Higher prices mean more profit per unit, so sellers want to sell more. At very low prices they may make a loss and pull back.

Law of supply. Ceteris paribus, when price rises, quantity supplied rises; when price falls, it falls. The supply curve therefore slopes upward. In the ice-cream example, sellers offer 2 cones at a price of 1.5 and 5 cones at a price of 3. Market supply is the sum of all sellers’ supply.

Shifts of a curve vs movements along it

The rule to remember

A change in the good’s own price moves you along the curve. A change in anything else that affects buyers or sellers shifts the whole curve. Right means more at every price; left means less.

What shifts demand

  • Income. For a normal good (a car) demand rises with income. If demand rises when income falls (taking the bus instead of buying a car), it is an inferior good.
  • Prices of related goods. Substitutes (ice cream and frozen yogurt): a cheaper one reduces demand for the other. Complements (ice cream and hot fudge): a cheaper one raises demand for the other.
  • Tastes and preferences. A fitness trend raises demand for protein shakes.
  • Expectations. Expecting a fuel price hike, people buy more petrol now.
  • Number of buyers. A new factory employing 2,000 people raises local demand.

What shifts supply

  • Input prices. Costlier inputs squeeze profit, so supply falls.
  • Technology. Automation and better processes lower cost and raise supply.
  • Expectations. If firms expect higher prices later, they hold back stock now.
  • Number of sellers. Sellers leaving the industry reduce capacity and supply.

The lecturer stressed that neither list is exhaustive.

Case: reducing cigarette demand

Policymakers can cut smoking in two different ways, and they look different on a graph.

Exam trap

“Demand fell because the price went up” is wrong wording. A price rise causes a fall in quantity demanded (movement). “Demand fell” means the curve shifted.

Equilibrium, surplus and shortage

Summary

The equilibrium price is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied: the point where the two curves cross. In a free market the price drifts back to it on its own.

Price above equilibrium → surplus

Sellers offer more than buyers want. Unsold stock builds up, so sellers cut prices until the market clears.

Price below equilibrium → shortage

Buyers want more than sellers offer. Sellers are under-motivated at the low price, so they raise it back towards equilibrium.

Case: a hot summer for ice cream

Summer raises demand at every price, so demand shifts right from D1 to D2 while supply is unchanged. The new equilibrium has both a higher price and a higher quantity: price rises from 2 to 2.5, quantity from 7 to 10. As an analyst you would plan seasonal pricing around this.

Price elasticity of demand

Summary

Elasticity is the degree of responsiveness of one variable to another. Price elasticity of demand (PED) asks: when price changes by some percentage, by what percentage does quantity demanded change?

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price |PED| > 1 elastic    |PED| < 1 inelastic    |PED| = 1 unit elastic

Worked example. The price of a cone rises 10% and the quantity you buy falls 20%. PED = 20 ÷ 10 = 2, so demand is elastic.

Sign convention

Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so PED is technically negative (−2). The lectures quote it as a positive ratio; compare its size with 1.

Two everyday contrasts from the lecture: if Netflix raises prices and many people cancel, its demand is elastic. If petrol prices rise and people still fill up, petrol demand is inelastic.

What makes demand more or less elastic

DeterminantMore elastic when…Less elastic when…
Close substitutesMany exist (butter has substitutes)Few exist, or the product is unique (eggs)
Necessity vs luxuryLuxury or nice-to-have (branded clothes, restaurants)Necessity (doctor’s visit, medicines)
Market definitionNarrowly defined (vanilla ice cream)Broadly defined (food)
Time horizonLong run: people find alternatives (petrol → EVs over years)Short run: no time to adjust

The five types

  • Elastic: a small price change causes a relatively larger change in quantity.
  • Inelastic: a price change causes a relatively smaller change, sometimes none.
  • Unit elastic: the two percentage changes are equal (10% price fall, 10% quantity rise).
  • Perfectly elastic: even a tiny price rise sends demand to zero, because buyers switch to identical substitutes. Typical of commodity, perfectly competitive markets (price moves 10 → 12 and sales collapse).
  • Perfectly inelastic: quantity does not change at all, whatever the price (life-saving insulin).
Why the lecture says “22%”

The unit-elastic graph moves price from 4 to 5 and quantity from 100 to 80, and the lecturer calls both changes about 22%. Simple percentages give 25% and 20%. The 22% figure matches the midpoint method: change ÷ average of the two values, so 1 ÷ 4.5 ≈ 22.2% and 20 ÷ 90 ≈ 22.2%. That method gives the same answer whichever direction you move, which is why textbooks use it.

Price elasticity of supply

Summary

Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how much quantity supplied responds to a price change. It is mostly about the flexibility of sellers: can they ramp production up or down quickly?

PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price

Worked example (milk). Price rises 10%, and dairy farmers raise output from 100 to 120 gallons, a 20% increase. PES = 20 ÷ 10 = 2: elastic supply. (The lecture says “20 divided by 10%”; the 20 is a percentage because the base is 100.)

What makes supply elastic or inelastic

FactorSupply is more elastic when…
Time horizonFirms have longer to adjust (a car maker can scale up over years, not weeks)
Spare capacityThe plant runs at 40–60%, not 95–98%
Availability of inputsRaw materials and skilled labour are easy to get
Nature of the productGoods are not perishable or unique (fresh produce and one-of-a-kind items tend to be inelastic)
TechnologyAutomation lets output scale fast
Government regulationLicensing is light (mining licences slow expansion and reduce elasticity)

The five types of supply

Elasticity changes along the curve for firms with limited capacity: elastic at low output (spare capacity is easy to use), inelastic near 90–98% utilisation.

Slip in the Lecture 7 transcript

The unit-elastic supply line says “a 10% fall in price leads to a 10% rise in supply. It is a perfectly elastic kind of situation.” Under the law of supply, a 10% price fall gives a 10% fall in quantity supplied, and unit elastic is not perfectly elastic. Treat this as a misspeak.

Case: a new high-yield wheat

  1. A hybrid crop raises yields, so the supply curve shifts right.
  2. Demand for wheat is inelastic and does not change.
  3. Equilibrium price falls a lot; quantity sold rises only a little.
  4. So farmers’ total revenue falls.

This explains the policy paradox of paying farmers not to plant: restricting supply raises price and, with inelastic demand, raises their revenue. Good news for farming can be bad news for farmers.

Case: OPEC in the 1970s

Short run

OPEC cut supply. Oil demand and supply are both inelastic in the short run (you cannot build extraction capacity overnight), so prices jumped and OPEC revenues rose.

Long run

Non-OPEC producers explored and built capacity; consumers conserved and bought efficient vehicles. Both curves became more elastic, and prices steadily declined.

The lesson: elasticity grows with time, so a strategy that works in the short run can unravel in the long run.

Economies of scale and scope

Economies of scale

Producing more of one thing lowers the average cost per unit, because fixed costs are spread over more units. Margins improve, prices can drop, and the supply curve shifts right. This is why firms offer bulk discounts.

Economies of scope

Producing a variety of related products with shared resources lowers cost. The same team serving three products instead of one spreads its cost three ways.

The two are separate ideas but converge: both lower cost, raise supply and build competitive advantage. Fixed and variable costs are covered properly in the later finance module.

Part B: Live session 1: production and cost

Graphs and slope

Summary

Graphs let you see patterns that are hard to read from equations or theories. Slope measures how responsive one variable is to another.

  • In maths, a graph usually has one independent input and one dependent output (y = mx + c: you choose x, get y). The lecturer called this a static plot, like land productivity plotted from 1970 to 2026.
  • In economics, the lecturer’s point was that variables influence each other (price affects quantity bought, and buying in bulk affects the price you get) and that the data is dynamic: prices change daily, even by the second for gold.

Two examples from class. Study time vs grades: more study hours, higher grades likely, so an upward-sloping line with positive slope (positive correlation). Party time vs grades: more partying, lower grades, so a downward-sloping line with negative slope (negative correlation).

Slope = Δy ÷ Δx = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)

Slope matters this week because marginal quantities (marginal product, marginal cost, MRT) are all slopes.

Framing to read carefully

The instructor said that in economics “both variables are dependent.” This is his way of stressing that price and quantity move together. Standard texts still treat price as the variable on the y-axis that quantity responds to; do not let this framing confuse you on an exam about the law of demand.

Cost of production, revenue and the production function

A firm turns inputs into output. Inputs include raw materials, machines, land, labour and a production facility. A tailor making T-shirts needs cotton, thread, a sewing machine and manpower. The money spent to acquire inputs is the cost of production.

Total revenue = quantity sold × selling price Profit = total revenue − cost of production

To maximise profit you can only work on these two levers, so you must know both.

Production function

The relationship between the inputs used and the maximum output they can produce. Written Q = f(K, L).

Farm example: one acre of land, one labourer working 2 hours a day, maximum output about 2.5 tonnes of wheat. Change the labour hours or the land and output changes.

The generalised form shown in class is the Cobb–Douglas production function:

Q = x₁α · x₂β

Here x₁ and x₂ are the two input variables, the factors of production, and α and β are constants. A student asked whether it is “x₁ into α”; the instructor confirmed it is x₁ to the power α. Only two factors are used because a 2D graph can show only two variables, not because real production has only two.

Notation
  • The instructor used K for labour and L for land in the farm example. In most textbooks K is capital and L is labour. Check which your exam uses.
  • The board writes the simple farm version as q = K × L. That is an illustration, not a standard formula.

Short run vs long run

Short run

At least one input is fixed. You cannot buy more land in two months, or move a cloud kitchen to a bigger space after three months of trading. You can still vary labour and equipment (buy a second oven, hire a helper). With land fixed, output depends only on labour: Q = f(labour).

Long run

Every input is variable. Planning five or ten years ahead, you can change land, buildings, staff and technology.

A second example used a student with a fixed four hours a day split between maths and statistics (2+2, 1+3 or 3+1): a fixed total input being re-allocated.

Marginal product and diminishing returns

Definition

Marginal product of an input is the change in output from one more unit of that input: MP = ΔQ ÷ Δinput. It is the slope of the output curve.

Law of diminishing marginal product (law of variable proportions)

When you keep adding a variable input (workers) to a fixed input (one shop, one oven), each extra unit eventually adds less output than the one before.

Why it happens: the fixed resources become crowded. In the pizza shop with one big oven, extra workers stand around waiting their turn, so system efficiency falls. A student put it well in class: workers share the same limited resources, so beyond a point new workers barely raise output.

Related idea, diminishing marginal utility: the instructor’s unlimited-pizza story. The first slices give great satisfaction; each further slice gives less, until you do not want pizza for two months. Satisfaction per extra unit consumed falls.

Connecting MP to cost

Each worker costs ₹500. So the extra cost of one more cookie is ₹500 ÷ MP: about ₹10 with the 1st worker, ₹12.5 with the 2nd, ₹16.7 with the 3rd, ₹25 with the 4th, ₹50 with the 5th and ₹100 with the 6th. Falling marginal product means rising marginal cost. These figures are my calculation from the class table, not stated in the session.

Part C: Live sessions 1–2: choices and profit

Scarcity, opportunity cost and sunk cost

The universal problem

Scarcity. Resources are limited, so you must make choices, and every choice is a trade-off (buy a car or go on vacation).

Scarcity + priorities and satisfaction = constant trade-off

For a data scientist the usual constraints are time, resources (computational power, tools, storage) and data.

Opportunity cost

The value of the next best alternative you give up when you make a choice. The instructor also called it the “ghost path”: the road you did not take.

  • Attending the economics session (option A) means giving up time on job assignments (option B). The value of B is the opportunity cost of A.
  • Studying a BS degree full-time means giving up a ₹15 lakh/year salary. Studying part-time while working might mean a reduced salary (₹12 lakh was the example) or lower performance in both.
  • A student’s example: a $200 trip vs a $50 night of Netflix at home. Choosing one gives up the other.
Sunk cost

Money already spent that cannot be recovered, whatever you decide next.

  • Degree fees already paid when you quit the programme.
  • A non-refundable flight ticket you then cancel.
  • An IPL team’s auction money for a player who underperforms. The team cannot get it back, but at the next auction it has a fresh choice.

Opportunity cost

A hidden cost of a choice still open to you. It belongs in decisions.

Sunk cost

A cost already lost. It should not change your future decision.

Case: choosing a fraud-detection model

A bank wants an AI model that predicts whether a loan applicant is a defaulter or eligible. The constraint is one month to build it, and the project pays ₹12 lakh. After research, the company has two options.

Option A: complex deep learningOption B: logistic regression
Accuracy95%85% on the board (80% spoken)
Time to build4 weeks1 week
ComputeExpensive GPUs, large storage, high computeMinimal
InterpretabilityBlack box: you cannot say why someone was flaggedHighly interpretable
Cost to deliver₹10 lakh₹5 lakh
Profit on ₹12 lakh₹2 lakh₹7 lakh

Choose A and you gain about 10 points of accuracy, but give up interpretability, three weeks of slack to refine the model, and budget: you forgo ₹7 lakh of profit to earn ₹2 lakh, a net ₹5 lakh worse.

Choose B and you keep time, money and interpretability, but lose about 10 points of accuracy. If the model misses defaulters, they get loans and the bank can lose lakhs of rupees.

The decision is a trade-off between your priorities (an accurate system or a cheaper, faster one) under scarcity. When a student asked how deep to go in such analysis, the instructor’s answer was: in real projects the client specifies the required accuracy and budget, so let the constraints guide how deep you go.

Accuracy figure

The transcript says option B is 80% accurate; the board says 85%. The instructor later talks of losing “10% of your accuracy”, which matches 85%. Use 85% unless your course materials say otherwise.

Explicit and implicit cost; economic vs accounting profit

Explicit cost

An actual cash outflow: money paid out to run the business. Wages, office rent, raw materials, cloud computing, software licences, the daily tea and coffee bill.

Implicit cost

The value given up that involves no cash payment. Your own time and effort (the salary you left to found the company), rent you could have earned on land you own and use yourself, your own laptop used for the business.

Two everyday examples from class. Buying flour for a cookie shop is an explicit cost, and the same money could instead have bought a machine, hired a worker or bought utensils. Making chapati has no cash cost for your time, but the hours spent collecting utensils, cleaning and making dough could have earned freelance income or been spent with friends. That forgone value is implicit.

Exam trap

Taxes and hidden fees (like a delivery app’s handling charge) are explicit: they are cash you pay, even if they are easy to overlook. “Implicit” means no money changes hands, not “hard to spot.”

EconomistAccountant

The instructor called this topic the profit illusion: a business can look profitable in the books while it is actually a worse use of your time and money than the alternative.

Worked case 2: a loss hidden by the books

A data consultancy earns ₹50,000/month, pays ₹20,000/month in rent and other expenses, and its founder gives up ₹50,000/month in alternative earnings.

Accounting profit = 50,000 − 20,000 = ₹30,000 per month Economic profit = 50,000 − (20,000 + 50,000) = −₹20,000 per month

The books show a profit, yet the founder would be ₹20,000 a month better off in the old job.

Worked case 3: assembled computers

A vendor is paid ₹4,000 per assembled computer (total revenue) and spends ₹1,500 on parts (explicit cost). Instead, he could teach assembly at an ed-tech STEM start-up for ₹2,000 (implicit cost).

Accounting profit = 4,000 − 1,500 = ₹2,500 Economic profit = 4,000 − (1,500 + 2,000) = ₹500

The instructor’s advice: run this analysis regularly (say every six months) and compare periods. And when a student asked how to put a number on time or effort, the answer was to price the alternative (₹5,000 an hour of freelancing, for example), while recognising that some decisions are strategic rather than purely mathematical. He mentioned game theory as where that thinking goes further.

Where “break-even” belongs

The board writes “break-even condition” under EP = 0, which matches standard economics. In the spoken transcript the instructor also says “break-even” while describing positive EP, meaning the firm has passed break-even. Remember: EP = 0 is break-even in the economic sense.

Production possibility frontier (PPF)

Definition, built the way the instructor built it

The PPF is a graphical representation of what? Of the possible combinations of goods or services a firm can produce. Why? To achieve maximum economic efficiency, that is, maximum use of available resources and so economic growth.

Also called the production possibility curve or transformation possibility curve. Inputs (land, machines, workers) are fixed. Producing more of one good means moving resources away from the other: a toy-and-cycle factory with 50 workers split 25/25 must decide how many toys to sacrifice for each extra cycle. A farmer splitting fixed land between wheat and corn faces the same question. Only two goods are shown because the graph is 2D.

Why the curve bends instead of being a straight line: resources are not equally good at both jobs. AI models need disproportionately more RAM and GPU (a student’s answer), and a dashboard expert moved onto AI work is slower than an AI specialist. A pizza expert asked to make burgers takes longer.

Opportunity cost, MRT and MOC on the PPF

Moving from C (2 models, 33 dashboards) to D (3 models, 25 dashboards) gains 1 AI model and sacrifices 8 dashboards. In money:

Gain: 1 × ₹15 lakh = ₹15 lakh     Given up: 8 × ₹2.5 lakh = ₹20 lakh Net effect of the move: −₹5 lakh
Marginal rate of transformation: MRT = ΔY ÷ ΔX Marginal opportunity cost: MOC = change in sacrifice ÷ change in gain

MRT is the exact number of dashboards that must be given up to make one more AI model at a given point. The slope of the PPF is the opportunity cost, so you can read it straight off the graph.

Which point is best?

The instructor described B (1X + 38Y) and E (4X + 14Y) as optimal combinations. More precisely, every point on the curve is efficient; which one is best depends on demand and prices. Using the class prices, revenue is ₹100L at A, ₹110L at B, ₹112.5L at C, ₹107.5L at D, ₹95L at E and ₹75L at F, so C would maximise revenue. Efficiency on the PPF

Efficiency = output ÷ input
PositionEfficiencyMeaning
On the curve100%No waste, full use of resources
Inside< 100%Underutilisation: idle or crowded workers (the overcrowded cloud kitchen), wrong skills in the wrong job. Persisting here can push economic profit negative.
Outside> 100%Over-utilisation: unachievable now. Needs extra resources, technical advancement or innovation.

Shifts and rotations

Rightward (outward) shift

More resources or better technology: upgrades, new investment, growth of resources. The instructor’s link: start-ups raise seed funding to push their PPF outward.

Leftward (inward) shift

Lost capacity: employees on leave or quitting, machine breakdown, a supply-chain failure. The firm can now produce fewer units of both goods.

Rotation: keep the resources for product Y fixed and change only product X’s capacity (or the reverse). The curve pivots around the fixed intercept, outward if X capacity grows and inward if it shrinks.

Assumptions behind any PPF analysis

  1. Inputs are fixed (a fixed plot of land, a fixed team).
  2. Resources are fully utilised.
  3. Technology and other conditions are constant.

If you do a six-monthly PPF analysis for a company, check these hold over the period.

Using the PPF in business analysis

  1. Identify the trade-offs: which products can be scaled down to free resources.
  2. Achieve maximum efficiency: reallocate inputs to stay on the curve.
  3. Evaluate the opportunity cost of each reallocation.
  4. Plan economic growth: what investment would shift the curve outward.

A working professional in the session summarised the payoff: if a planned product mix falls inside the curve, you can tell before spending anything that it will not make money.

SUMIF and SUMIFS

One condition    =SUMIF(range, criteria, sum_range)
                 =SUMIF(A19:A29, "Tablet", P5:P15)

Many conditions  =SUMIFS(sum_range, criteria_range1, criteria1, criteria_range2, criteria2)
                 =SUMIFS(P5:P15, A19:A29, "Tablet", B19:B29, "East")
Argument order trap

In SUMIF the range to add comes last. In SUMIFS it comes first. Mixing these up is the most common error with these functions.

The flight-data working sheet (about 10,700 flights) uses SUMIFS to total ticket price by source and destination:

As in the file   =SUMIFS(K6:K10688, C6:C10688, P6, D6:D10688, Q6)

Safer            =SUMIFS($K$6:$K$10688, $C$6:$C$10688, P6, $D$6:$D$10688, Q6)

Because the ranges in the file are not locked with $, each copied row starts one row lower and silently drops a flight from the top. Lock the data ranges and leave only the criteria cells (P6, Q6) relative.

Loan EMI with PMT, IPMT and PPMT

Example in the file: principal ₹50,00,000, 12% annual interest, 20 years.

EMI                       =PMT(B6/12, B7*12, B5)
Interest in month n       =IPMT(B6/12, n, B7*12, B5)
Principal in month n      =PPMT(B6/12, n, B7*12, B5)
Check                     IPMT + PPMT = PMT
  • Divide the annual rate by 12 and multiply years by 12, because payments are monthly.
  • Results come out negative because Excel treats a payment as cash flowing out.
  • EMI ≈ ₹55,054. In month 1, interest = ₹50,000 (1% of ₹50 lakh) and principal repaid ≈ ₹5,054. Early EMIs are mostly interest.

VLOOKUP

=VLOOKUP(lookup_value, table_array, col_index_num, [range_lookup])
=VLOOKUP("880-10032", $A$2:$E$8, 2, FALSE)    → Last name for that ID

Use FALSE (or 0) for an exact match. The lookup value must be in the first column of the table.

The basic VLOOKUP sheet needs fixing

The practice formulas in VLookUp (Vertical Lookup) omit the fourth argument, so Excel does an approximate match on IDs that are not sorted, and the table range shifts in each column (A2:F8, B2:G8 …). They look like work in progress. The correct pattern is =VLOOKUP($A12, $A$2:$E$8, 2, FALSE), changing only the column number.

Advanced: let the formula pick the column

From Excel_Advanced_VLOOKUP_Formula.xlsx:

Across a row   =VLOOKUP($A$4, $A$7:$H$37, COLUMN(), 0)
Down a column  =VLOOKUP($M$1, $A$7:$H$37, ROW(), 0)

COLUMN() returns the current column number (2 in column B, 3 in C …) and ROW() the current row number. Laid out to line up with the table, one formula copied across or down returns First name, Last name, Department and so on with no manual column numbers. Type a new employee code in A4 or M1 and the whole record updates. The trick depends on the layout: move the formulas and the column numbers break.

Practice questions

Try each one before opening the answer.

A café raises coffee prices by 8% and sells 4% fewer cups. Is demand elastic or inelastic, and what happens to revenue?

PED = 4 ÷ 8 = 0.5, which is less than 1, so demand is inelastic. Revenue rises, because quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rises.

The government taxes cigarettes and the price rises. Separately, it runs anti-smoking ads. Which causes a shift of the demand curve?

The ads. They change preferences, so demand shifts left. The tax changes the price, which is a movement along the existing curve.

In the cookie table, what is the marginal product of the 4th worker, and why is it smaller than the 3rd’s?

140 − 120 = 20 cookies per hour (the 3rd added 30). With the shop and oven fixed, extra workers share the same equipment, so each adds less: diminishing marginal product.

TFC = 20, TVC = 18 at Q = 2 and 10 at Q = 1. Find ATC at Q = 2 and MC from Q = 1 to Q = 2.

TC at Q = 2 is 38, so ATC = 38 ÷ 2 = 19. TC at Q = 1 is 30, so MC = (38 − 30) ÷ 1 = 8.

The idli shop’s rent is ₹15,000 a month. What is AFC at 2,000 packets?

15,000 ÷ 2,000 = ₹7.50 per packet.

You quit a ₹10 lakh job to run a business with ₹12 lakh revenue and ₹3 lakh explicit costs. Find accounting and economic profit, and the implication.

Accounting profit = 12 − 3 = ₹9 lakh. Economic profit = 12 − (3 + 10) = −₹1 lakh. Negative economic profit means your resources would earn more elsewhere; in the long run, exit or change the business.

You bought a non-refundable ₹6,000 concert ticket, then get a free invitation to something you would enjoy more. Should the ₹6,000 affect your choice?

No. It is a sunk cost: gone whichever event you choose. Compare only what each option gives you from now on.

On the AI/dashboard PPF, what is the MRT moving from B to C?

ΔY = 33 − 38 = −5 dashboards, ΔX = +1 model. MRT = 5 dashboards per AI model (−5 with sign).

A firm’s actual output plots inside its PPF. What does that tell you, and what might cause it?

Resources are underutilised (efficiency below 100%). Causes include idle or crowded workers, people with the wrong skills on a task, or machines standing unused.

Why does a hybrid wheat that boosts harvests lower farmers’ total revenue?

Supply shifts right while demand for wheat is inelastic. The price falls by a larger percentage than quantity sold rises, so P × Q falls.

Indian telecom has fewer than ten operators. Which market structure is that, and who influences price?

Oligopoly. The few large players have high bargaining power, so prices are not set purely by free-market forces.

Write a formula to total column P where column A is “Speaker” and column B is “West”.

=SUMIFS(P:P, A:A, "Speaker", B:B, "West"). With SUMIFS the sum range comes first.
