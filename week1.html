Needs, wants, desire, demand

Definition. Demand is desire backed by the ability and willingness to pay. A firm only counts you as demand if you have purchasing power.

Infinite wants

  • Humans have infinite (insatiable) wants: once needs are met we keep wanting more. The lecturer notes this is a widely held belief, not something established by scientific testing.
  • This does not make people selfish: we also care about others and wish them to have more.
  • “Wants” here means goods and services that people desire and that improve quality of life. Week 3 covers the difference between a product and a service.

Scarcity and the economic problem

Key idea. If wants were finite, or resources unlimited, there would be no economic problem and no economics. Neither is true, so choices must be made.

Ways the lectures define economics

  • The science of constrained choices. Video 1
  • The science of allocating scarce resources to meet unlimited human wants. Video 1
  • How goods and services are produced and delivered, and why individuals, businesses and governments make the spending decisions they do. Video 2
  • The study of a system: understand it, analyse it, then optimize it. Live 2
  • Optimization under constraints. Live 2 (recap)

The economic problem is the need to allocate scarce resources carefully because we cannot all get everything we want.

Economic goods and free goods

  • Economic goods are scarce: producing them uses scarce resources.
  • Free goods exist in enough supply to meet every want (the example is air).
  • Not paying does not make something free. Free hospitalization for poor people is still an economic good, because scarce resources are used to provide the healthcare.

Resources and the economic problem in Video 1

  • Resources come from “Mother Earth” and are grouped into four factors of production: land, labour, capital, enterprise (details in Production).
  • Production in economics is any activity that satisfies a human want; goods and services have been produced once they are supplied.

Two common misconceptions

  • “Economics is about money.” Money is studied only because it helps address the economic problem. Resources can be time, supply chains, anything scarce. Video 1Live 1
  • “Knowing economics guarantees business success.” It will not make you rich or poor, but it helps you understand business situations and decide better. Video 1

Economics for data work

Dr. Chandel opened with a question: why study economics in a data science programme? Live 1

Two myths about data

  • Myth: data science means only coding, analytics and plotting x against y.
  • Reality: data is raw information from any field (finance, medicine, images), containing pattern plus noise. You extract the pattern that matters for this company’s domain and discard the noise.

When asked, students defined data as a collection of facts or information, raw and unstructured (or semi-structured) until organized. The board adds: BDM = Business + Data + Management.

Key idea. Nobody pays you to plot x and y. They pay for a data-centric solution: an explanation of what is happening, a view of what will happen, and a sensible action. Knowing a theorem is useless if you do not know where to apply it.

What a data scientist brings, and what is still missing

  • Skills: managing computational resources, advanced algorithms (LLMs, machine learning, reinforcement learning), and cleaning data.
  • Missing without domain concepts: the ability to give reasons. A live CPU-usage chart is a set of spikes. Without hardware knowledge you cannot say why usage hit 65%, or how to improve performance.
  • Multiple factors matter: a batter is judged on runs, strike rate and innings played, not one number (the Virat Kohli example).

Data, machine learning, economics

Example: dynamic pricing. A delivery app sees far more orders than riders. Demand cannot be met, so it prices higher or prioritises premium customers. Data shows the shortage, ML forecasts how prices will move, and economics (demand, supply, equilibrium) explains the mechanism and the options. Prices change every second, so this needs algorithms, and the model must keep prices acceptable: if surge pricing is too high, customers go to a competitor.

  • A supply-chain break pushes commodity prices up, as in floods or the recent geopolitical disruption the instructor cited.
  • Map and compass: locations on a map are the data; economics is the compass that tells you how to reach the one you want.

The whiteboard also sketches a demand curve and a supply curve crossing at the equilibrium point, where buyers are willing to buy and sellers are willing to sell. Demand and supply come in week 2 and later live sessions.

The three concepts doing the work

Elastic and inelastic

Elastic customers change what they buy when price (or value) changes. Inelastic customers do not. “Value” is wider than money: time counts too.

Trade-off

Choosing one thing means giving up another. A student with a fixed refreshment budget (say ₹20 a day) who keeps coffee may drop the samosa. Another story: a friend with two movie tickets and a quiz tomorrow must sacrifice something.

Scarcity

Unlimited wants meet a limited budget (for example ₹1,000 pocket money a month), so you aim for maximum satisfaction within it.

Scarcity inside a data project

  • With unlimited computing power accuracy could be very high, but it would cost a lot. Real projects negotiate: reduce data quality or size, or settle for an accuracy the client accepts.
  • If the client is happy with 95%, you run on what you have. If they demand 99%, you need more resources (for example a different GPU). Live 1
  • A company may aim to maximize utility, minimize cost or increase savings, and uses mathematical modelling such as cost-benefit analysis to pick the option closest to that objective. Video 2

Key idea. Scarcity is a constraint. “Economics is about money” is a myth: the thing being allocated can be money, time, supply or compute.

Micro, macro, and economics vs economy

Microeconomics (bottom-up)

From Greek “micro”, small. Individual consumers, businesses, workers and their financial decisions. Think zooming in with a camera lens, or one tree in a forest.

  • IPL franchises want the best players, but squad size and budget cap what they can buy.
  • A person who works more has less leisure time.
  • Effect of tariffs or supply disruption on consumer behaviour and businesses.
  • One company’s data: Swiggy’s recommendations from your order history, Ola’s surge pricing, TCS, a single shop’s sales in one town.

Macroeconomics (top-down)

From Greek “macro”, large. The economy as a whole. Think zooming out, or the health of the whole forest.

  • GDP, inflation, unemployment.
  • Effect of tariffs or geopolitical tension on a country’s inflation, GDP and employment.
  • A government giving education more budget and infrastructure less; steering the economy with taxes.
  • Successful UPI transactions per day across India.
  • They are interconnected, not separate subjects. Constrained optimization appears in both, and the decision-maker must choose the best option within the constraints.
  • BDM focuses on microeconomics, since you will mostly solve a company’s problem. Macro is mainly about policy-level decisions. Live 2
  • Households sell their labour to firms and earn money; firms depend on those people to produce what they sell. Video 2

Utility and marginal thinking

Video 2 Economics is rooted in a few fundamental ideas: scarcity, utility, and marginal thinking.

Check this. Video 2 first lists the foundations as “scarcity, utility and marginality”, but later introduces the third as “rationality”. Both ideas appear in the lecture, so I cover them under Utility and Rational choice.

Definition. Utility is the satisfaction a consumer gets from consuming goods or services. It influences economic decisions and differs between people: two people who buy the same coat do not get equal utility. Someone in a cold climate who wears it often gets more than someone in a warm climate who wears it rarely.

Marginality and marginal utility

  • Marginality: people rarely make economic decisions all in one go. They decide bit by bit.
  • Marginal utility (MU): the extra satisfaction from consuming one more unit.
  • Law of diminishing marginal utility: as consumption rises, the MU of each extra unit falls, and it can turn negative if you overconsume.

Example. The first ice cream (or the first slice of pizza when you are hungry) gives the most pleasure. The second gives less, because you have tasted it or feel full. Keep going and satisfaction goes below zero.

Assumptions behind the law

  • Items consumed are identical (the same ice cream each time).
  • Units are consumed quickly, with few breaks.
  • The consumer is rational.
  • Items can be measured.
  • Taste stays constant.
  • Units are neither too big nor too small.

The lecturer said he was listing “only a few”, so this may not be the full set.

Why it matters for pricing

Backpack store. One backpack costs ₹300, two cost ₹550, three cost ₹750. Everyone who wants a backpack needs at least one, so the first is priced highest. Few buyers want three even at a good deal. Because marginal utility drops with each extra unit, the store lowers the per-unit price to persuade people to buy more. Keeping the price flat would let diminishing marginal utility stop them buying.

My addition The extra cost of the second backpack is ₹250 (550 − 300) and of the third is ₹200 (750 − 550): each additional unit is priced lower.

Marginal utility equals marginal cost

The lecture ends this topic by noting that as quantity rises, marginal utility falls while marginal cost rises. Where the two meet, the customer gets no more satisfaction from additional consumption. Problems on this were promised for the live sessions, so the detail is not covered in the recordings. Video 2

Rational choice, opportunity cost, specialization

Rationality and cost-benefit analysis

  • People are “rational animals”: decisions mostly follow a thought process, although impulse buying exists.
  • Individuals and businesses choose when benefits outweigh costs, and may not choose when costs outweigh benefits.
  • Organizations often score options on weighted criteria (price, product quality, service quality, brand image) and pick the highest rating. Week 3 returns to this.

When the rule bends: few alternatives

  • In B2C there are many alternatives, so switching costs are low and customers can move brands easily.
  • In B2B, or under a monopoly, alternatives are few and switching costs are high, so choices do not follow the simple cost-benefit rule as neatly.

Opportunity cost

Definition. When you make one choice you give up the others. The cost associated with those lost opportunities is the opportunity cost. A quote credited to Dan Ariely: every time you spend money on something, you cannot spend it on something else.

Groceries. A nearby shop sells at MRP; a supermarket is a 15 to 20 minute drive away.

  • Buy nearby: the extra money you pay could have been used elsewhere.
  • Drive to the supermarket: the time spent could have been used elsewhere.

Which is better is a subjective cost-benefit judgement. One person values time; another enjoys the discounts, variety and ambience.

  • Spending 150 hours preparing and recording lectures, or ₹1,000 on a movie, each has an opportunity cost.
  • Produce vs outsource: producing costs time and resources that could go elsewhere; outsourcing costs money that could go elsewhere. A firm decides by modelling against its objective.
  • Even attending a session has an opportunity cost: the time you could have spent on something else. Live 2

Specialization and division of labour

People earn money by specializing in particular tasks (farming, nursing, making furniture). This is the division of labour, and it underlies the four economic sectors in Production. Video 2

Economic systems

Every economy has to answer the same questions (what, when and how to produce), but who answers them differs. Live 2

Command (socialist, centrally directed)

  • A rule-based system, like if/else code. Central authorities (government) decide what to produce, when and how, and you may not add a rule without permission.
  • Rule example: if demand > 100 kg a day, produce 50 more; else halt production. The same idea applies to T-shirts or other goods.
  • Examples named: the Soviet Union, and India before the 1990s, when licences controlled production.
  • Drawbacks (from students and the instructor): rigid, little incentive to compete or innovate, the same service for everyone, and slow to adapt to change.

Free market (capitalist)

  • Companies and owners decide prices, discounts and innovation; the government has no role in these decisions, and competition forces firms to respond to each other.
  • Reliance entered telecom in 2003 with free incoming calls; rivals had to cut prices to keep customers (the “3.5 million customers in one day” figure is the instructor’s claim).
  • Swiggy personalising offers and refunds; a vegetable vendor cutting the price for bulk buys; phone makers setting prices by features.

Mixed economy

Delivery in 10 minutes. Firms competing to promise 10-minute delivery was a purely company decision, so a capitalist one. As accidents rose and delivery partners’ work-life balance suffered, the government (the Supreme Court, as the instructor told it) pushed for rules limiting such claims. That is a government “gate” on a free market, for social welfare, which makes the system mixed.

  • Banking: RBI controls interest rates and account-opening documents (KYC) to reduce fraud, keep the sector stable and protect customer trust. NPCI oversees UPI, and wallets need KYC.
  • Public-sector firms (BHEL, HAL, NTPC) have a government stake alongside management. The board uses an illustrative 40% government and 60% management split.
  • India today is mixed: innovation is free, but regulated in places.

Open vs closed economy

Open

Anyone can come in, invest and trade. Resources, technology and investment flow across the border.

Closed

No exchange across the boundary. Everything is made inside, including raw materials.

  • Drawbacks of closed: developing technology alone is slower and more expensive. Compare learning machine learning with no courses or videos, which could take years instead of months.
  • Advantages (raised by a student): self-sufficiency, immunity to global supply shocks (for example a closed strait), and technology kept secret. The instructor agreed there are advantages and disadvantages.
  • India once restricted foreign investment; Walmart entered through acquiring Flipkart, as the instructor recounted.

The transcript names a closed-economy example, but the country name is garbled, so I have left it out.

Economics as a social science

  • In science, results are deterministic: 1 + 1 = 2 everywhere, and the acceleration due to gravity is 9.8 m/s² regardless of location.
  • In social science, responses change with geography, age, gender and mindset. Ask 61 students for an example of a free market and you get different answers. Rice is grown differently in Tamil Nadu, Punjab, Assam and Gujarat.
  • That is why economics has no single concrete answer to many questions. The optimization part, allocating resources under constraints, is the more “purely scientific” part.

Production: chain, outputs, factors, sectors

Three elements of economics

Production, distribution and consumption. Economics studies how to optimize this chain when raw material is limited but demand must still be met. Live 2

Toothbrush case. Ancestors cleaned teeth with neem sticks. As technology and travel changed, demand created innovation: a normal toothbrush in the 1990s, many shapes (zigzag, flexible) in the 2000s, electric ones in the 21st century. Firms then confirm what customers need by taking continuous feedback. A company that sticks to traditional ways risks losing customers, which is previewed by an IBM case study to come.

  • The study of the production-to-consumption chain is economics; the chain in operation is the economy. Within it you look at relationships between variables, for example how raw material changes over time (steadily, non-linearly, or fluctuating).
  • Resource allocation in practice: during a fuel shortage the instructor noticed no queues in a large city but closed pumps in a smaller town. His reading: the government prioritised big cities, where industry and logistics cannot stop and alternatives are few, while smaller places could use bicycles or firewood. This is his observation, not a documented policy.

The three central problems

  1. What to produce: what does the market demand?
  2. How to produce: which resources and processes (for example, how to produce biodiesel)?
  3. For whom to produce: who are the potential customers? Many startups build a product without knowing where to sell it.

Market, production and outputs

  • A market is where you, me and others exchange goods and services, to fulfil needs or to earn a profit. Payment can be money, but it can also be a swap of goods.
  • Production turns raw material into a usable form: cotton into T-shirts, shirts or sarees; tea leaves into tea.
  • Outcomes are goods (visible, tangible: you can see, touch and store them) or services (invisible, intangible: you can only experience them). A milkman supplies milk (a good) and the delivery (a service).

Factors of production

  • Land: scarce natural resources such as oil or sea (Video 1); also the site or leased premises (Live 2).
  • Labour: people available to work, with knowledge and intelligence. In economics “labour” is simply the standard term for workers.
  • Capital: goods used to produce other goods, such as buildings and machinery (Video 1). Live 2 splits it into fixed (long-term) and working (short-term) capital.
  • Enterprise / human capital: the entrepreneurs and managers who combine and run the other three.

Worked problems

Both problems follow the same recipe: pick the variables, write the constraint equation, fix what is given, and solve for what is left. Live 2

Problem 1: Ramu’s budget

Given. Ramu sells tea (T) and filter coffee (F). He spends ₹1,200 a day on raw materials.

  • Tea: costs ₹6 a cup to make, earns ₹4 profit a cup.
  • Filter coffee: costs ₹12 a cup to make, earns ₹6 profit a cup.

A campus workshop orders 40 filter coffees. He can take the order or refuse it.

Constraint: 6T + 12F = 1200 Order of 40 coffees: 12 x 40 = 480 (spent on coffee) Left for tea: 1200 - 480 = 720 Teas he can make: 720 / 6 = 120 cups Profit: 4 x 120 + 6 x 40 = 480 + 240 = Rs 720 Check: 6 x 120 + 12 x 40 = 720 + 480 = 1200 (budget fully used)

The whiteboard writes the tea variable as C in the middle of the working, but it is the same quantity (tea). I use T throughout.

My addition Each coffee costs twice what a tea costs (12 ÷ 6), so every extra coffee means two fewer teas. That 2-for-1 swap is the trade-off (opportunity cost) built into the constraint.

Key idea (preview of the PPF). On the line: resources fully used, efficient. Inside it: resources under-used, so efficiency and profit fall (the “undershooting” case). Outside it: you would need more resources such as labour or machines (“overshooting”). The PPF gets its own session next week.

A student asked what happens if regular tea demand is higher than the budget allows. The instructor: serve what the constraint allows (some customers may be missed), and if the demand is persistent, expand the resources (more labour, machines or budget). Leaving the budget unused wastes stock such as milk.

Problem 2: the organic farm

Given. A farm grows basmati rice (B acres) and wheat (W acres).

  • Profit: ₹15,000 per acre of basmati, ₹8,000 per acre of wheat.
  • Water: a government limit of 10,000 litres a day from the tube well. Basmati uses 500 L per acre a day, wheat 200 L per acre a day.
  • Soil: to maintain fertility (nitrogen), at least 5 acres of basmati must be grown (mixed farming).

Find how many acres of wheat fit, and the total profit.

Constraint 1 (water): 500 B + 200 W = 10,000 Constraint 2 (soil): B = 5 acres (minimum, taken as fixed) Water used by basmati: 500 x 5 = 2,500 L Water left: 10,000 - 2,500 = 7,500 L Wheat acres: 7,500 / 200 = 37.5 acres Profit: 15,000 x 5 + 8,000 x 37.5 = 75,000 + 3,00,000 = Rs 3,75,000 (Rs 3.75 lakh)

Check this. The problem never states the farm’s total land, so I do not know whether 5 + 37.5 = 42.5 acres actually fits. The lecture solves only the water and soil constraints.

My addition Why using exactly 5 acres of basmati is the best split. Profit per litre of water is ₹30 for basmati (15,000 ÷ 500) and ₹40 for wheat (8,000 ÷ 200). Wheat earns more per litre, so once the minimum basmati is met, the remaining water should go to wheat.

Self-check

Try answering each aloud before opening it.

What is the difference between a want, a desire and demand?

A want improves quality of life once needs are met. A desire is an emotion-driven want that may lack affordability. Demand is desire plus ability and willingness to pay.

Why would there be no economics if wants were finite?

Resources would be enough to meet them, so no scarcity and no need to allocate carefully. Economics exists because wants are unlimited and resources are scarce.

Is free hospitalization a free good?

No. It is an economic good, because scarce resources are used to provide it even though the patient does not pay.

Name the three kinds of scarcity with one example each.

Demand-induced (population growth raises demand, supply unchanged), supply-induced (a fuel supply-chain disruption or crop failure, demand unchanged), structural (villages having less access to healthcare and services than tier-one cities).

What do data, machine learning and economics each contribute?

Data: what is happening. ML: what will happen. Economics: why it is happening and what to do about it.

In Ramu’s stall, why did samosa sales fall?

Inelastic customers kept buying coffee at the higher price, but with a fixed budget they traded off the samosa. The percentages are illustrative.

What two things does every optimization problem need?

An objective function and constraints.

State the law of diminishing marginal utility and one business use.

As you consume more, the extra satisfaction from each unit falls (and can go negative). Stores lower the per-unit price for bulk purchases to keep marginal utility, and sales, up.

Give an example of opportunity cost in the grocery case.

Buying nearby: the extra money paid could have gone elsewhere. Driving to the supermarket: the time could have gone elsewhere.

Classify: a government licence for every product; a firm setting its own prices; banks regulated by RBI.

Command; free market; mixed.

Land, labour, physical capital, entrepreneurship. Primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary.
